P, Q, R Branch Origin

The vibrational transition $v=0\to1$ is accompanied by simultaneous rotational changes ($\Delta J = \pm 1$, plus $\Delta J = 0$ when allowed). With $J$ the lower-state quantum number and band origin $\tilde{\nu}_0 = \omega_e - 2\omega_e x_e$:

$$\tilde{\nu}_P = \tilde{\nu}_0 - (B_1+B_0)J + (B_1-B_0)J^2$$ $$\tilde{\nu}_R = \tilde{\nu}_0 + (B_1+B_0)(J{+}1) + (B_1-B_0)(J{+}1)^2$$ $$\tilde{\nu}_Q = \tilde{\nu}_0 + (B_1-B_0)J(J{+}1)$$

Line intensities: $R(J) \propto (J+1)\,e^{-E_J/k_BT}$ and $P(J) \propto J\,e^{-E_J/k_BT}$ (Hönl–London factors), so the R branch is slightly stronger than the P branch.

Why No Q Branch for HCl?

HCl, HBr, HF and CO have $^1\Sigma^+$ ground states: no electronic orbital angular momentum about the bond axis ($\Lambda = 0$). The photon carries one unit of angular momentum, which must change the rotation, so $\Delta J = \pm 1$ only — the Q branch ($\Delta J = 0$) is forbidden.

NO is also linear, but its $^2\Pi$ ground state has $\Lambda = 1$, so $\Delta J = 0$ is allowed and a (weak) Q branch appears near $\tilde{\nu}_0$. Linear polyatomics also show Q branches in perpendicular bands (e.g. the CO₂ bend $\nu_2$).

Vibration–Rotation Coupling (αe)

In $v=1$ the anharmonic bond is longer on average, so $\langle 1/r^2 \rangle$ is smaller and the rotational constant decreases ($B_1 \lt B_0$):

$$B_v = B_e - \alpha_e\left(v + \tfrac{1}{2}\right), \qquad \alpha_e = B_0 - B_1$$

Because $B_1 - B_0 < 0$, R-branch lines crowd together at high $J$ while P-branch lines spread apart. Centrifugal distortion ($-DJ^2(J+1)^2$, with $D \approx 5\times10^{-4}$ cm⁻¹ for HCl) is a separate, much smaller effect and is neglected here.

Temperature Effects

At higher temperatures, higher $J$ levels become more populated (Boltzmann distribution), so lines at larger $J$ grow in intensity and the band broadens.

The most populated level: $J_{\max} \approx \sqrt{k_BT/2hcB_0} - \tfrac{1}{2}$.
